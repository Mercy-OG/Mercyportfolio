Nigeria's HIV Epidemic at a Glance

Despite a relatively low national prevalence, Nigeria's large population means it carries the largest HIV burden in West and Central Africa and the 4th highest globally by absolute numbers.

Why were different sources used? Nigeria has never had a single continuous HIV tracking system. The 2001 to 2013 data came from sentinel surveillance at antenatal clinics, which tends to give higher estimates. The 2018 figure came from NAIIS, the first nationally representative household survey and the most accurate. The 2022 figure is a Bayesian statistical model, a mathematical projection based on known trends, not a new national survey.

Has HIV actually come down? Yes, significantly. The drop from 5.8% (2001) to 1.4% (2018) is real, driven by ART rollout, prevention programmes and better data accuracy. The 2022 model estimate of 2.1% reflects projected growth after the sharp 2018 drop, but is not a measured figure. The core truth is: prevalence has fallen, treatment has expanded, but new infections continue daily.

The Treatment Gap: Where the System Is Failing

UNAIDS set the 95-95-95 targets: 95% of PLHIV know their status, 95% of those on ART, 95% of those virally suppressed. Nigeria's current position shows how far the gap still is.

Nigeria 2024 Progress: ~72% know status. 90% of diagnosed are on ART. Only ~42% of all PLHIV are virally suppressed. This means over 1.1 million people living with HIV in Nigeria are still able to transmit the virus.

Key Field Observations: What the Data Cannot Show
Stigma and Denial
Across all community outreaches, just one person acknowledged their HIV status out of everyone we met. The exception was hospital visits, where some patients had already disclosed. In the open community, denial remains the primary barrier to engagement.
Youth Disengagement
Secondary school students showed clear discomfort with HIV discussions, treating it as a "sex topic." Many wanted sessions to end quickly. Yet 40% of new infections are in the 15 to 24 age group.
Older Adults
Some older community members deflected to requesting condoms rather than engaging with education. A form of acknowledgment without acceptance: they know the risk but avoid the conversation.
Rural Access Gap
Etim-Ekpo LGA has limited hospital infrastructure, with primary health centres serving as the main points of care. While PHCs provide a frontline presence, they often lack specialist capacity for HIV management. People living with HIV face real access barriers that simply do not exist for those in Uyo or Port Harcourt.
